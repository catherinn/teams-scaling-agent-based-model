Team Scaling

Scaling up or scaling down, every change costs your team output. See how much.

⬆ Scaling up

New people need months to ramp up, and every joiner sets their team back. Teams split as they grow, and coordination eats more time. Hire too fast and output per person collapses.

⬇ Scaling down

Layoffs remove more output than headcount. Knowledge leaves with people, teams are merged, survivors lose motivation and more people quit.

How the model works

People ramp up

New hires start at 20% (junior) or 40% (senior) productivity. Seniors reach full speed in about 6 months. Juniors reach 60% in about 6 months, then grow to full speed over roughly 2 years. Ramp-up slows when a team has more newcomers than experienced people to learn from.

Teams gel, and change resets them

Every team moves through forming, storming, norming and performing (Tuckman, 1965) in about 10 months. Every person who joins or leaves knocks the team back. Splitting a team that got too big, or merging teams that got too small, knocks it back further.

Bigger means more overhead

Communication paths grow as n(n−1)/2, so a team's output peaks at around 10–12 people (diminishing returns, Ricardo 1817; Brooks 1975). Coordinating many teams costs extra on top.

Layoffs cost more than salaries

Leavers take knowledge with them, which their teammates must rebuild. After a layoff, survivors lose motivation for about a year and more people leave voluntarily. The chart compares this with the same company without the layoff.
